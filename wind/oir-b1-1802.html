The inspection form · OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26)

What does Florida’s wind-inspection form actually record, and who is allowed to sign it?

The current form is OIR-B1-1802, revision 04/26. Inspections on or after April 1, 2026 must use it. It records the home’s building code, roof, and opening-protection features. Only certain licensed people — or someone the insurer recognizes — may sign it. A completed form can stay valid for up to five years if the house has not materially changed and no inaccuracies are found.

What the form is

The official name is the Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form. Florida law, in section 627.711, Florida Statutes (2026), requires a single state form that insurers must accept when a policyholder submits it so the insurer can factor insurance discounts for wind coverage. “Mitigation” on the form means storm-protection features on the house — roof connections, opening protection, and the like. On this site we usually say “insurance discount” for the reduction that may follow, not “mitigation credit,” unless we are quoting the law.

The Florida Department of Financial Services (DFS) says the Office of Insurance Regulation approved a new version, OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26), and that it “must be used for all inspections as of April 1, 2026.” The new form, DFS says, “is more comprehensive with additional categories and specifies the documentation that must be used to verify entries on the form.” Inspections before April 1, 2026, may use the older form, OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 01/12), which had an effective date of February 1, 2012.

DFS and the form itself say a completed form “is valid for up to five (5) years provided no material changes have been made to the structure or inaccuracies are found on the form.”

What the Rev. 04/26 form actually records

The official form is six pages. It starts with the inspection date, the owner’s information, the year of the home, the number of stories, and the insurer and policy number if you have them. The inspector then answers nine numbered questions. This list is a plain-language map of those questions, not a substitute for the form.

Question on the form What it is asking, in plain words
1. Building Code Which code was in force when the original permit was applied for: the 2001 and 2004 Florida Building Code, the 2007 Florida Building Code or later, or — in Miami-Dade or Broward, the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone — the 1994 South Florida Building Code. There is also a box for “unknown” or “does not meet” those choices. Homes from certain transition years need a permit-application date after a date printed on the form.
2. Region Where the house sits by design wind speed, using ASCE 7-22: the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, Region 1 (140 mph or more), Region 2 (130–139 mph), or Region 3 (under 130 mph).
3. Roof Slope For a single-family home, whether the slope that covers at least two-thirds of the main roof is 6:12 or steeper, or less than 6:12.
4. Roof Covering Every roof-covering type in use (for example asphalt/fiberglass shingle, tile, metal, membrane), plus a permit date or a Florida / Miami-Dade product-approval number and the year of installation or replacement — or a mark that no compliance information was available.
5. Roof Deck Attachment The weakest way the roof deck is fastened. Options range from lighter nailing patterns to heavier nailing, a concrete roof deck, certain spray-foam applications, “other,” “unknown,” or “no attic access.”
6. Roof to Wall Attachment The weakest roof-to-wall connection, not counting certain hip or valley jacks near a corner. The form’s main labels are toenails, clips, single wraps, double wraps, structural (including a reinforced concrete roof), “other,” “unknown,” “no attic access,” or connections not installed as intended.
7. Roof Geometry The roof shape. A “hip roof” on this form means a hip roof with no other roof shapes greater than 10% of the total roof-system perimeter. A “flat roof” option applies only to certain buildings with five or more units. Anything else is “other roof.” Porches or carports attached only over unenclosed space are left out of that measurement.
8. Sealed Roof Deck / Secondary Water Resistance Whether there is a sealed roof deck (also called secondary water resistance) that can help if the roof covering comes off. The form says standard underlayment or hot-mopped felts do not qualify. It lists specific membranes, taped seams, a double layer of certain felts or synthetics, and certain spray-foam applications.
9. Opening Protection The weakest wind-borne debris protection on the structure — windows, doors, sliding glass doors, skylights, and garage doors, not gable or other roof vents. The form grades verified impact-resistant products, certain plywood or OSB panels meeting a 2007 Florida Building Code table, unverified coverings, no protection, or damaged openings that need repair or replacement. Buildings with openings that need replacement, the form says, “do not qualify for wind mitigation rate differentials,” even if other features are present.

The form’s own note says any documentation used to validate a feature must accompany the form, and that at least one photograph or document providing proof must accompany the form for each attribute marked in questions 2 through 9. A FORTIFIED Home certificate (Roof, Silver, or Gold) may be used to validate the attributes marked in questions 4 through 6 and 8 through 9, if the certificate type is checked and a photocopy is attached. The insurer may ask more questions about the features on the form.

Each page of the form also repeats: “Some items listed may not qualify for a discount.” The last page adds that the definitions on the form “are for inspection purposes only and cannot be used to certify any product or construction feature as offering protection from hurricanes.”

Source: Office of Insurance Regulation, Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form, OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26), adopted by Rule 69O-170.0155, F.A.C., opened November 19, 2026, America/New_York.

Who is allowed to sign it

Section 627.711(2) says an insurer shall accept as valid a uniform form signed by one of the following authorized inspectors:

  • A home inspector licensed under section 468.8314, Florida Statutes, who has completed at least 3 hours of hurricane-mitigation training approved by the Construction Industry Licensing Board — including hurricane-mitigation techniques and compliance with the form — and has completed a proficiency exam. The DFS forms page also says that, after that, licensed home inspectors must complete at least 2 hours of continuing education each year related to the inspection and the form. That yearly 2-hour rule appears on the DFS page we opened; it is not in the text of section 627.711 itself.
  • A building code inspector certified under section 468.607, Florida Statutes.
  • A general, building, or residential contractor licensed under section 489.111, Florida Statutes.
  • A professional engineer licensed under section 471.015, Florida Statutes.
  • A professional architect licensed under section 481.213, Florida Statutes.
  • Any other individual or entity recognized by the insurer as possessing the necessary qualifications to properly complete the form.

The same statute says an insurer may, but is not required to, accept a form from any other person whose qualifications and experience are acceptable to the insurer.

The person authorized to sign must inspect the structure personally, not through employees or other people, and must attest to that personal inspection. There is one stated exception: licensed professional engineers (section 471.015) and licensed contractors (section 489.111) may authorize a direct employee — not an independent contractor — who has the needed skill, knowledge, and experience to conduct the inspection. The insurer may ask the authorized inspector for information about that employee’s qualifications before accepting the form. The inspector who certifies the form is responsible for the employee’s work.

The form also has a line for the homeowner (or an authorized representative) to sign, certifying that the named inspector, or the inspector’s employee, did inspect the residence and that proof of identification was provided.

DFS notes that, at the insurer’s expense, the insurer may require the form to be independently verified before accepting it as valid. That right is also in section 627.711. Citizens Property Insurance’s public inspection page says its underwriters review the form and supporting documents, and that a form may not be accepted if the inspection was not done by the appropriate professional or if that professional’s Florida license is not active or cannot be verified. If your insurer is Citizens, use Citizens’ own instructions.

Sources: Fla. Stat. § 627.711 (2026); DFS, Mitigation Notices, Inspections and Forms; Citizens, Your Wind Mitigation Inspection; all opened November 19, 2026, America/New_York.

What Citizens says you should have ready

Citizens’ public page is not a rule for every insurer, but it is a clear official description of a typical inspection. It says attic access is required to verify certain features, and that if the inspector cannot get into the attic you may not be eligible for discounts for roof-to-wall attachment, roof-deck attachment, or secondary water-resistance features. It also says you may designate someone 18 or older to be there if you cannot attend, and that this person should know where the attic access is and where any stored storm shutters or panels are. Citizens lists examples of useful documents: receipts or product specifications, work orders and invoices, roofing permits, and notice-of-acceptance letters for mitigation products.

Before you send the form

  • Confirm the form version. For an inspection on or after April 1, 2026, DFS says the inspector must use OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26).
  • Confirm the signer is on the statutory list — or is someone your insurer recognizes — and that the Florida license is active. You can look up many licenses at MyFloridaLicense.com, which the DFS consumer guide points to.
  • Ask whether the inspector will attach photos or other proof for the features marked in questions 2 through 9, as the form requires.
  • If features are in the attic, make sure the attic can be entered. Citizens says missing attic access can block some discounts. Other insurers may have the same practical limit even if they word it differently — ask yours.
  • Keep a copy of the form and the attachments with the insurance policy, as the form itself instructs.
  • Send the completed form to your insurer and ask that company, in writing, what insurance discount it will apply. This page cannot answer that.
