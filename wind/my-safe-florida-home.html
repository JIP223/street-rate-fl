Grants · My Safe Florida Home

What is My Safe Florida Home, who can get a grant, and is the program accepting applications today?

My Safe Florida Home is a Florida Department of Financial Services program. It offers eligible homeowners a free hurricane-mitigation inspection and, for those who also qualify, a grant toward certain recommended storm-protection work. The official applicant portal has been temporarily unavailable starting November 10, 2026, during a system upgrade. Official sources expect it to reopen December 4, 2026. This is not insurance advice, and a grant is not a guarantee of an insurance discount.

Current application and funding status

We opened the official program site, mysafeflhome.com, on November 23, 2026, America/New_York. The site returned an official notice that the program is upgrading its system and that “the applicant portal will be temporarily unavailable starting November 10th while we move to the new, modernized platform.” The page linked to the program’s Modernization FAQs.

Those official FAQs, modified November 6, 2026, and opened by us on November 23, 2026, say the portal closure is expected on November 10, 2026, and that reopening is expected on December 4, 2026. They say there is a temporary period beginning November 10 during which new grant applications cannot be accepted. They also say already-approved inspections can still be scheduled, already-approved grants may continue eligible work, and applicants are not bumped in line. The FAQs say people will get an email notice when the new portal is available, and they list program support at 850-427-2559, Monday through Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time. The official wording is “expected,” not a promise that the portal will reopen on that date.

Official sources do not all say the same thing about whether applications are open, or why they are closed, so we are not going to smooth that over:

  • An older official support article, “Is funding currently available?”, last modified August 17, still says the program is now accepting applications. That article does not describe the November portal closure.
  • The official 2025-26 Program Terms PDF, still served from the program site and opened by us on November 23, 2026, includes a note that the program “is currently closed to new Inspection and Grant Applications, as program funds have been fully allocated due to high demand,” and tells people to check the website for updates. That PDF does not date that closure note, and the later official notices describe a November 2026 system upgrade.

As of November 23, 2026, the most recent official notices we opened describe a temporary portal outage for a system upgrade, with an expected reopening on December 4, 2026. We could not confirm, from a clearly dated official funding statement opened today, whether new applications will be funded the day the portal reopens. Check mysafeflhome.com for the current status.

What the program is

Section 215.5586, Florida Statutes (2026), places the My Safe Florida Home Program in the Department of Financial Services. The official 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide, updated July 21, 2025, and opened by us on November 23, 2026, says the program has two parts: a hurricane-mitigation inspection, provided free of charge and with no obligation, that identifies hurricane-resistant features and recommended improvements; and a hurricane-mitigation grant to help pay for specific improvements recommended by that inspection.

The 2026 statute says the program is not an entitlement and is subject to annual appropriations. The 2025-26 Program Terms say a free inspection does not guarantee a grant, and that grants are paid only after eligible work is completed.

The same statute says the initial inspection report must include recommended improvements, “a range of cost estimates regarding the recommended mitigation improvements,” and estimated insurance-premium discounts. Those estimates are for the inspected house. They are not a statewide price list.

Who can get the free inspection

The 2026 statute says a homeowner may have a qualified inspector inspect for the presence of attributes that reduce hurricane damage. To be eligible for an inspection, the home must be a site-built, owner-occupied, single-family unit on an individual parcel that is either detached or attached and not exceeding three stories, and the applicant must have a homestead exemption under chapter 196. The statute also says a homeowner may have an inspection without also being eligible for a grant, and that each eligible applicant may have one inspection per homesteaded residence.

The 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide, which cites section 215.5586, describes the same idea in program language: a site-built, owner-occupied single-family detached house or a townhouse, as defined in section 481.203, Florida Statutes, with a homestead exemption. The guide says attached single-family homes will be treated as townhouses for purposes of the program. It also lists properties that are not eligible for the free inspection: retirement homes; mobile homes and manufactured homes; and second homes, vacation homes, and rental properties.

The wording is not identical. The 2026 statute uses “attached residential property not exceeding three stories.” The July 2025 guide uses “townhouse” and treats attached single-family homes as townhouses. When the portal reopens, follow the current official application rules, and use the 2026 statute if you need the current law.

The official 2025-26 Program Terms define an inspection application as a request for a “free Hurricane Mitigation Inspection.” The Homeowner’s Guide says an approval for an inspection is not an approval for a grant, and that each applicant can receive only one initial inspection for the current homesteaded property.

Who can get a grant

Under section 215.5586(2), Florida Statutes (2026), an applicant is eligible for a hurricane-mitigation grant if all of the following are met:

  • The applicant is eligible for an inspection under the statute.
  • The home is a dwelling with an insured value of $700,000 or less.
  • The home had an initial program inspection within the 24 months immediately preceding the grant application.
  • The home “must have been built before January 1, 2008, as reflected on the county property appraiser’s website.”
  • The applicant agrees to make the home available for a final inspection once the project is completed.
  • The applicant agrees to give the department information from the insurer identifying discounts realized because of the improvements funded through the program.
  • The applicant is a low-income person or moderate-income person as defined in section 420.0004, Florida Statutes.

The 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide, updated July 21, 2025, uses a different construction-date test in two places: it says the building-permit application for initial construction must have been made before January 1, 2008, and later that the home’s initial building-construction permit must have been issued before January 1, 2008. The 2026 statute uses the property-appraiser record of a home built before January 1, 2008. We are not choosing one unofficially. The current statute is the 2026 text linked below; the guide is the program’s 2025-26 homeowner document. Confirm how the program is applying the rule when you apply.

The 2025-26 guide also says insurance documentation must show an insured value of $700,000 or less under Coverage A (Dwelling). It says townhouses are only eligible to receive funding for opening-protection improvements, and that attached single-family homes will be treated as townhouses for the program. It says applicants who receive no recommended improvements, or who are in Group 5 (applicants of all ages above the moderate-income threshold), are not grant-eligible. The 2026 statute requires low- or moderate-income status for a grant, and it lists “all other applications” last only for inspections.

We are not reprinting income dollar limits here. They vary by area and household size and can change. The 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide points applicants to the official HUD income-limits page, huduser.gov/portal/datasets/il.html, to check income eligibility. The 2025-26 Program Terms also define low-income homeowners by reference to section 420.0004(11) and list “80% of median income” as the income threshold for low-income classification.

Grant amounts and match rules

The 2026 statute is direct. All grants “must be matched on the basis of $1 provided by the applicant for $2 provided by the state up to a maximum state contribution of $10,000 toward the actual cost of the mitigation project,” except for low-income applicants. Low-income applicants, as defined in section 420.0004(11), “are eligible for a grant of up to $10,000 and are not required to provide a matching amount.”

The 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide says the same match in program language: the matching grant is a reimbursement, the program provides $2 in grant funds for every $1 contributed by the applicant, and the applicant is eligible to be reimbursed for two-thirds of the total costs of the mitigation project, up to a state contribution of $10,000, subject to legislative appropriation. The 2025-26 Program Terms list “$10,000” as the maximum state contribution for matching grants and low-income grants, and they say low-income homeowners are not required to provide matching funds or a paid-in-full invoice.

The Homeowner’s Guide and an official support article include example calculations using $9,000, $15,000, and $20,000 project totals for recommended impact-rated windows. Those figures are examples of the match math. They are not published typical prices for windows, and we do not treat them as market costs. The related costs page explains what official sources do and do not say about prices.

Both the guide and the statute require that grant-funded work be recommended by the initial inspection and identified by the final inspection. The guide says a grant application must be approved before any work begins, and that performing work, signing a contractor contract, or purchasing materials before receiving an email from the program telling you to proceed will result in a denial. Payment is requested later through a Draw Request. The 2025-26 guide says each applicant may receive only one grant disbursement and one final inspection, and cannot reapply.

On timing after approval, the official sources differ, so both are dated here. The 2026 statute says an applicant who receives a grant “shall finalize construction and request a final inspection within 18 months after grant application approval,” or the application is deemed abandoned and the grant money reverts. The 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide says funding is set aside after approval, and that the applicant must request a final inspection, or request a 6-month extension, within one year of grant-application approval; if no recommended improvements are completed within that year, the application is administratively closed and funds are reallocated. Follow the current official instructions when you have an approved grant.

What a grant can pay for

When the initial inspection recommends them, the 2026 statute allows grants for opening-protection improvements — exterior doors, garage doors, windows, and skylights — and for roof improvements: reinforcing roof-to-wall connections, improving the strength of roof-deck attachments, and installing secondary water resistance for the roof and replacing the roof covering. All work must be based on required local permits and inspections and must be performed by properly licensed contractors. The grant application must include the intended contractor’s name and state license number, which the program must verify.

The official program documents group those same items as four improvements: opening protection; roof-to-wall attachment; roof-deck attachment; and secondary water resistance. Official support articles and the 2025-26 guide say only the specific openings recommended in the initial inspection report are eligible, and that the grant cannot be used to exchange one type of opening protection for another. If compliant hurricane shutters are already in place, a request to pay for impact windows on those openings will be denied. See hurricane shutter and impact window costs for the opening-protection rules and permit basics.

How to apply

When the portal is available, the 2025-26 Homeowner’s Guide says you go to mysafeflhome.com and click “Access Account.” You first submit a prioritization questionnaire. Your group determines when an application will be available to you. The guide says that if the program runs out of available funding before your group’s date, you will not be able to apply for an inspection or a grant.

The 2026 statute says the department must review and approve inspection and grant applications in this order: first, low-income persons who are at least 60 years old; second, all other low-income persons; third, moderate-income persons who are at least 60 years old; fourth, all other moderate-income persons; and last, all other applications for an inspection. After a legislative appropriation, those groups may begin 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 days after the program starts accepting applications.

The guide says you must receive an initial inspection report from the program before applying for a grant. After approved work is finished, you request a final inspection through the portal, then a Draw Request for disbursement. The guide says a local building inspection is separate from the program’s final inspection, and that each applicant has only one opportunity to request a final inspection.

As of November 23, 2026, the official site and the Modernization FAQs say the applicant portal is temporarily unavailable and that new grant applications cannot be accepted during that period. Review the official guide, and watch the official site for the reopening notice, rather than starting from an older “now accepting applications” article.

Before you rely on this page

  • Check the official site today. Portal and funding status can change. Start at mysafeflhome.com.
  • A free inspection is not a grant. Official documents say so in plain terms.
  • Do not start grant work early. The 2025-26 guide says work, contracts, or material purchases before the program tells you to proceed will result in a denial.
  • Ask your insurer separately about any discount. A completed improvement or a program inspection does not, by itself, tell you what your premium will be. See how the insurance discounts work and the official inspection form.
